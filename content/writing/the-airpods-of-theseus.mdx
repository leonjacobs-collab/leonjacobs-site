Theseus had a boat. Well, he called it a ship, but we all know how humans of my gender exaggerate. It could have just been a boat.

Apparently, this vessel gave him a lot of trouble. So much so that he had to replace many parts over time. You can imagine him showing up at the shipyard and the mechanics already grinning, ready to shake their heads and whistle as they inspected whatever had broken this time.

And, what often happens when things in our lives disturb our peace is that we become philosophical. This ship gave Theseus so much grief that he thought some deep thoughts. Eventually, after replacing so many parts, he wondered: if I replace all of them, would this still be my ship?

---

For some reason, Theseus' ship came to mind as I was standing in the Apple shop repair queue a few days ago, clutching the little white case of Airpods Pro that goes everywhere with me. One of the buds will no longer charge, and I was at the modern shipyard, waiting for the Apple Genius to whistle and shake his head.

I thought back to the many cases and buds that have cycled through my life and lightened my wallet over the years. The broken charging contacts. The one bud that just gave up. The scratches on the case that no amount of care could prevent. Each one a small maintenance cycle, each one a decision: spend money to keep them alive, or let them go.

And I realized Theseus was asking the wrong question.

---

He was asking what makes something *itself*. But the real question — the one that matters — is simpler: does it still work?

These AirPods aren't precious because they're original. They're precious because they *work*. Because I use them. Every day, probably. They're the soundtrack to my thinking. They're isolation from the world when I need it. They're the ability to be in my own head while moving through a coffee shop or a street or a meeting where I need to disappear into focus. They enable me to sit at my open plan desk and go from meeting to meeting, without moving in space.

When a bud breaks, I don't mourn the loss of the original object. I just fix it. Because the utility matters. The function matters. The fact that they still let me think, still let me listen, still let me have a moment alone in public — that's what makes them mine.

Theseus preserved his ship as a monument. But I don't need my AirPods to be a monument. I just need them to keep playing.

So yes, if I replace every part, they're still my AirPods. Not because of some philosophical continuity of identity, but because they still do what I need them to do. Because I still live with them. Because maintaining them is proof that I value the utility, not the object.

The value was never in the pristine original. It was in the fact that they still work. That I can still think. That I can still have a soundtrack to my life without being interrupted.

And maybe that's the only philosophy that actually matters.
